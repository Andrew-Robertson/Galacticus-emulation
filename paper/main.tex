\begin{abstract}
Forward modelling the \romanspace{} Grism Survey requires mock galaxy catalogues that are simultaneously realistic in stellar mass, star formation, size, metallicity, black-hole demographics, and emission-line luminosity.
We present a calibrated \galacticus{} semi-analytic model designed for this purpose.
Because direct likelihood exploration with \galacticus{} is computationally expensive, we construct Gaussian-process emulators of binned observable predictions from a Sobol training campaign, compressing each observable with principal-component analysis where appropriate.
We validate the emulators using held-out \galacticus{} runs, calibrate the model to a suite of low- and intermediate-redshift galaxy observables, and then extend the calibration to include observed \ha{} luminosity functions.
The final calibrated model reproduces the calibration data at the level required for Roman mock-catalogue generation.

\section{Introduction}

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will carry out a wide-field near-infrared grism survey over cosmological volumes, providing galaxy redshifts for large-scale structure measurements and a large sample of emission-line galaxies for studies of galaxy evolution \citep{2015arXiv150303757S, 2022ApJ...928....1W}.
These measurements will extend galaxy redshift surveys to large samples at redshifts where the expansion history and growth of structure are less tightly constrained by existing galaxy surveys.
This is especially timely given recent DESI baryon-acoustic-oscillation (BAO) measurements.
DESI first-year BAO data are consistent with $\Lambda$CDM on their own, but when combined with CMB and Type Ia supernova data they show a mild preference for time evolution of the dark energy equation of state \citep{2025JCAP...02..021D}.
This is not yet conclusive evidence for evolving dark energy, but it does sharpen the case for independent measurements of the distance--redshift relation and growth history.
Roman is valuable in this context because it will probe these quantities at high redshift, with a different instrument, galaxy selection, and set of observational systematics from existing ground-based spectroscopic surveys.

Roman will obtain these redshifts in a very different way from a targeted spectroscopic survey such as DESI \citep{2016arXiv161100036D}.
In DESI, robotic fibre positioners place fibre-optic cables on pre-selected galaxies and quasars, so that the spectrograph receives light only from those chosen locations on the sky.
The Roman grism survey will instead be \emph{slitless}: it will disperse the light from the full field of view, and will therefore obtain spectra for essentially every source that is bright enough to contribute detectable flux.
This is a powerful feature of the survey, because it avoids a hard preselection of targets and can in principle provide spectra for all sufficiently bright sources in the field.
It also makes the data more complicated to interpret.
Because the spectra are dispersed images of the galaxies, spatial and spectral information are mixed together, spectra from neighbouring objects can overlap, and the detectability of an emission line depends not only on its total flux but also on the size and morphology of the galaxy.

The redshifts used for the Roman grism survey will be obtained primarily from strong rest-frame optical emission lines, especially \ha{} and \oiii, with \oii{} also relevant at higher redshift.
For Roman mock catalogues, the line luminosities must be connected to the underlying galaxy population: stellar masses, star formation rates, gas metallicities, dust attenuation, galaxy sizes, and host halo environments.
These correlations matter both for cosmological analyses, where the number density and bias of selected tracers are important, and for image-level simulations, where source sizes, line fluxes, continuum emission, and blending determine whether a redshift can be recovered.

There are several possible ways to build such mocks.
Empirical models such as the \textsc{UniverseMachine} \citep{2019MNRAS.488.3143B} provide a powerful way to connect galaxies to dark matter halos, and newer empirical frameworks such as \textsc{diffsky} extend this approach to star-formation histories and spectral-energy-distribution modelling.
These models are extremely useful, but they are designed to be flexible descriptions of the galaxy--halo connection rather than direct calculations of the baryonic processes that regulate galaxy formation.
Semi-analytic models offer a complementary route.
In a semi-analytic model, galaxies are evolved along dark matter halo merger trees using approximate, physically motivated prescriptions for baryonic processes \citep[e.g.][]{2006RPPh...69.3101B, 2015ARA&A..53...51S}.
These models are far less expensive than hydrodynamical simulations, but still encode explicit calculations for gas cooling, star formation, feedback, metal enrichment, galaxy sizes, black-hole growth, and nebular emission.
For Roman mock catalogues, this is attractive because the same model can be asked to predict many correlated properties of each galaxy.

In this paper we calibrate a \galacticus{} galaxy-formation model intended both for Roman grism mock generation and for broader applications requiring physically motivated, self-consistent galaxy catalogues.
We make use of a broad set of calibration data because they constrain different aspects of the galaxy formation model. We focus on low and intermediate-redshift calibration data, because these measurements are typically better understood than the corresponding high-redshift constraints.
At the same time, we include \ha{} luminosity functions at higher redshift, because emission-line galaxies are central to the Roman grism survey and because a model calibrated only to the lower-redshift galaxy population is not necessarily adequate for this application.

\galacticus{} is much less expensive than a hydrodynamical simulation, but it is still too expensive to run directly inside an MCMC analysis.
In \citet{2026OJAp....955306R}, we tried a different acceleration strategy: rather than evolving a large ensemble of merger trees, and the galaxies within them, for every likelihood evaluation, we calibrated \galacticus{} using small sets of galaxies at a few fixed halo masses.
That approach is most natural for quantities for which there are observational estimates of the galaxy distribution at fixed halo mass, with stellar mass being the clearest example \citep[e.g. observationally-inferred stellar-to-halo mass relations from][]{2012ApJ...744..159L, 2019MNRAS.488.3143B}.
The resulting calibration produced a good match to the observed stellar mass function.
One might hope that matching stellar mass functions at several redshifts would also constrain the predicted star-formation rates to match observations, because the model must form the correct amount of stellar mass between those redshifts.
In practice, this is not sufficient: the integrated amount of star formation can be approximately correct even if the instantaneous distribution of star-formation rates is not.
The model in \citet{2026OJAp....955306R} therefore matched stellar mass functions more successfully than \ha{} luminosity functions.

For the Roman grism survey this distinction matters, because the redshift sample is built from emission-line detections.
Here we therefore use full \galacticus{} runs to compute the observables that enter the likelihood, including emission-line luminosity functions, but replace repeated direct model evaluations with an emulator.
We construct this emulator from a finite training set of \galacticus{} runs, test its accuracy with held-out cross-validation runs, use it to sample the posterior distribution of the model parameters, and then check representative posterior models with direct \galacticus{} evaluations.

\section{Model And Emulator Training Set}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The \galacticus{} Model}

We use \galacticus{}, a semi-analytic model of galaxy formation \citep{2012NewA...17..175B}.
For a given dark matter halo accretion and merger history, encoded in the so-called \emph{merger tree} \citep{1993MNRAS.262..627L}, \galacticus{} follows the formation and evolution of the galaxies associated with each halo and subhalo.
The calculation includes parameterised prescriptions for gas cooling, star formation, stellar feedback, AGN feedback, galaxy sizes, galaxy mergers, black-hole growth, and metal enrichment.

The fixed modelling choices used here are mostly the same as in \citet{2026OJAp....955306R}. By fixed modelling choices, we mean choices such as the prescriptions for disk star formation, gas cooling, black hole accretion, and stellar and AGN feedback. Compared with \citet{2026OJAp....955306R}, we vary a substantially larger set of numerical parameters within this model, as described in Section~\ref{sec:varied-parameters}. We therefore focus here on the fixed modelling choices that differ from \citet{2026OJAp....955306R} and on the parameters varied during the calibration. The \galacticus{} code is open source, and the complete parameter files used in this work will be made publicly available alongside the paper.\footnote{\todo{Add repository/version and parameter-file URL or Zenodo DOI.}}



The main fixed model choice that differs from \citet{2026OJAp....955306R} is the treatment of galaxy sizes.
In that work, galaxy component sizes were computed using an iterative equilibrium calculation, in which the baryons contribute to the gravitational potential used to determine component radii.
In the larger-volume runs needed here, this typically produced a small sub-population of galaxies with unrealistically small sizes, with scale lengths roughly 10--100 times smaller than typical galaxies of the same stellar mass.
We therefore use a simpler size calculation, in which component radii are determined from their specific angular momentum in the dark matter halo potential, without allowing the baryons to modify that potential.\footnote{Specifically, \citet{2026OJAp....955306R} used
\texttt{galacticStructureSolverEquilibrium},
whereas here we use
\texttt{galacticStructureSolverSimple};
see the
\href{https://galacticus.readthedocs.io/en/latest/physics/galacticStructureSolver.html\#galacticstructuresolverfixed}{Galacticus documentation}.}
%The origin of the small-size tail is not yet fully understood, but one possibility is that the \emph{pseudo-angular-momentum} treatment used for spheroid sizes is being applied outside the regime where it is physically appropriate, given that these systems are not typically rotationally supported.

\subsection{Merger Trees And Halo Sampling}
\label{sec:tree-sampling}

For the training runs, \galacticus{} is run on extended Press--Schechter merger trees.
We evolve the trees down to $z=0$, where they contain 17,385 distinct host halos over the mass range $10^{11}<M_{\rm halo}/\msun<3\times10^{14}$.
Their masses follow a Sheth--Tormen halo mass function \citep{1999MNRAS.308..119S}, with $a=0.791$, $p=0.218$, and normalisation $0.302$, the parameter values from \citet{2019MNRAS.485.5010B}.
Each of these $z=0$ host halos contains a central galaxy and may also host satellite galaxies. The calculation follows the complete merger tree of each $z=0$ halo, including all progenitor branches.
The abundance of host halos over this mass range corresponds to an effective comoving volume of $V_{\rm eff}\simeq 1.3\times10^6\,\mpc^3$, equivalent to a cubic box with side length $\simeq 109\,\mpc$.
Because the calibrated model is intended to generate Roman mocks using the UNIT simulations \citep{2019MNRAS.487...48C}, we use a merger-tree mass resolution of $2\times10^{10}\,\msun$, similar to the minimum halo mass resolved in UNIT.
For the same reason, the merger trees are rebinned onto the UNIT simulation output times, following the approach of \citet{2012MNRAS.419.3590B}.

The use of extended Press--Schechter trees is mainly a practical choice.
It gives direct control over both the number of simulated halos and their mass distribution, which is useful when the goal is to build smooth training data for mass functions, size relations, emission-line luminosity functions, and other binned summary statistics.
In Section~\ref{sec:nbody-comparison} we include a comparison with a calculation based on merger trees extracted from the UNIT $N$-body simulation \citep{2019MNRAS.487...48C}, to assess the size of the effect for selected observables.

\subsection{Calibrated Galacticus Parameters}
\label{sec:varied-parameters}

Our aim is to calibrate a model suitable for mock-catalogue generation, for which accurately reproducing a broad range of observed galaxy properties is more important than reserving observables as independent tests of the model. We therefore vary 20 \galacticus{} parameters and constrain them using several galaxy observables simultaneously.

This differs from the calibration strategy often adopted for cosmological hydrodynamical simulations such as EAGLE, IllustrisTNG, and COLIBRE \citep[e.g.][]{2015MNRAS.446..521S, 2018MNRAS.475..624N, 2026MNRAS.548ag375S}, where a relatively small set of observables is typically used for calibration, leaving other galaxy properties as stringent tests of the model. While that is a valuable objective, it is not the primary goal of this work. Our 20 free parameters control aspect of gas cooling, star formation, stellar feedback, black-hole seeding, accretion and feedback, reincorporation of ejected gas, the classification of major and minor mergers and the sizes of merger remnants, angular-momentum transfer during disk instabilities, and the stellar metal yield. \subsection{Emulator Training Set}
\label{sec:emulator-training-set}

The emulator training set consists of $N_{\rm train}=1024$ \galacticus{} model evaluations, with parameter values drawn from a scrambled Sobol sequence \citep{1967USSR...7...86S, 1997AnSta..25.1541O}.\footnote{The design was generated with \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol}, using \texttt{scramble=True}, \texttt{seed=42}, and \texttt{random\_base2(m=10)}.}
Compared with random sampling, this provides a more uniform coverage of parameter space, reducing the likelihood of large unsampled regions and generally improving emulator accuracy for a fixed number of model evaluations.
We generate Sobol points in the unit hypercube and transform each coordinate through the inverse cumulative distribution function of the corresponding parameter prior.
As a result, the training density follows the prior density specified in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}, while retaining the space-filling properties of the Sobol design in prior-quantile space.
The choice of 1024 training evaluations represents a compromise between reducing emulator error across the 20-dimensional parameter space and keeping the computational cost of the \galacticus{} runs manageable.
Section~\ref{sec:smf-example} uses the low-redshift stellar mass function as a concrete example to illustrate how emulator accuracy changes with training-set size.

\subsection{Emission-Line And Dust Post-Processing}
\label{sec:dust-postprocessing}

To connect the calibrated galaxy population to Roman grism observables, we augment the standard \galacticus{} outputs with emission-line luminosities and dust attenuation.
The training campaign runs sidecar post-processing for emission-line luminosity functions using the \texttt{emission\_line\_dust} products.

\subsection{Set Of Calibration Observables}
\label{sec:calibration-observable-set}

Each \galacticus{} evaluation produces a catalogue of galaxies at a set of
output times. From these galaxies we compute binned observable vectors, such as
stellar mass functions, luminosity functions, size--mass relations, and scaling
relations. The emulator is trained to reproduce these vectors as a function of
the varied \galacticus{} parameters.

The final calibration uses the observable set listed in
Table~\ref{tab:calibration-observable-summary}. For number-density observables
we denote the binned differential number density by $\Phi$, with the argument
indicating the quantity being binned, for example $\Phi(M_\star)$ for a stellar
mass function and $\Phi(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha})$ for an \ha{} luminosity function.
The final column of the table gives the transformed quantity passed to the
emulator and used in the likelihood. This is also the space in which the
emulator-validation RMSE values in Table~\ref{tab:emulator-validation-summary}
are measured. For example, an RMSE of 0.1 for a luminosity or mass function
means 0.1 dex in $\log_{10}\Phi$, not a linear number-density error.

For each model evaluation, we also estimate the uncertainty on the predicted observable vector. This uncertainty arises because each observable is constructed from a finite simulated galaxy population, with the relevant uncertainty model depending on the observable being analysed. \subsubsection{Observable-Space Transformations}

For observables that span a large dynamic range across \galacticus{} parameter
space, we emulate logarithmic quantities.  For number-density observables this
means emulating $\log_{10}\Phi$ rather than $\Phi$ itself.  At fixed stellar
mass, star formation rate, or emission-line luminosity, changes in the
\galacticus{} parameters can produce order-of-magnitude changes in the
predicted number density.  The logarithmic transformation therefore gives a
smoother emulator target across parameter space and makes emulator errors more closely related to fractional differences in $\Phi$, matching the scale on which luminosity
and mass functions are usually compared.

We use the same approach for other positive quantities with broad dynamic
range.  The size--mass relations are emulated as $\log_{10}R_{\rm eff}$ and
the black-hole scaling relation as $\log_{10}M_{\rm BH}$.  The gas-phase
mass--metallicity relation is already conventionally expressed as
$12+\log_{10}({\rm O/H})$, and is emulated in that form.

Some training-set predictions are undefined after these transformations. For
example, a model may produce no galaxies in a stellar-mass-function bin, so that
$\log_{10}\Phi$ is undefined, or no quiescent galaxies at
$M_\star \simeq 10^{11.2} \msun$, so that the median half-light radius in that bin
is undefined. These cases arise when an observational bin is included in the
calibration data but a particular \galacticus{} model produces no corresponding
galaxies, most often at the high-mass or high-luminosity end and in sparsely
populated subsamples. We therefore replace non-positive number densities by a small positive floor before taking the logarithm. For undefined binned quantities such as median sizes, we replace the missing value with a representative value derived from the valid training-set predictions and assign it a large training uncertainty, so that the assigned value carries very little weight when fitting the emulator. The detailed floor and missing-value procedure is described in Appendix~\ref{app:missing-values}.


\subsubsection{Principal-Component Compression}

One possible approach would be to build a separate Gaussian process for every bin of every observable: for example, one emulator for the number density of galaxies at $M_\star\simeq10^{10}\msun$, another at $M_\star\simeq2\times10^{10}\msun$, and so on.
This is straightforward, but it treats neighbouring bins as independent targets even though changes in \galacticus{} parameters usually move the predictions coherently across an observable.
For example, a change in feedback strength may shift the amplitude, slope, or knee of a stellar mass function, rather than perturbing each stellar-mass bin independently.

We therefore use principal-component analysis (PCA) to compress each observable vector before emulation.
This reduces the number of Gaussian processes that must be trained and evaluated, and can also improve performance when the training set is sparse because the emulator is asked to learn the dominant coherent modes of variation rather than noise or poorly constrained bin-to-bin fluctuations.
PCA--GP emulators are commonly used for expensive simulation outputs with many correlated bins, including recent applications to cosmological and galaxy-formation inference \citep[e.g.][]{2026arXiv260107306R}.
For a given observable, let
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
  =
  \left(y_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}), \ldots, y_{N_{\rm bin}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)
\end{equation}
be the vector of model predictions in the bins used for that observable, after applying any pre-processing used for the emulator.
For the low-redshift stellar mass function, for example, $y_b(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log_{10}\Phi_b(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, where $\Phi_b$ is the predicted number density in the $b$th stellar-mass bin.
For each \galacticus{} run in the training set we have one such vector, so the training data for this observable form an $N_{\rm train}\times N_{\rm bin}$ matrix.
We standardise each bin using the training-set mean and scale, fit a PCA basis, and retain the number of components required to explain 99 per cent of the training-set variance.
The emulator therefore predicts the PCA coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \simeq
  \boldsymbol{\mu}
  + \mathbf{S}
  \left[
    \mathbf{m}_{\rm PCA}
    + \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathbf{e}_k
  \right],
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ are the bin-wise standardisation vectors, $\mathbf{m}_{\rm PCA}$ is the PCA mean in standardised space, $\mathbf{e}_k$ are the retained PCA components, and $a_k$ are the PCA coefficients.

\subsection{Gaussian-Process Emulators}
\label{sec:gp-regression}

Each retained PCA coefficient is modelled with a Gaussian process as a function of the input parameters.
The same basic GP choices are used for the emulators compared below, so that the validation tests mainly probe the effects of training-set size and PCA compression.
The GP inputs are not the physical parameter values themselves, but the corresponding prior quantiles.
That is, for parameter $\theta_i$ with prior cumulative distribution function $P_i(<\theta_i)$, we train the emulator using
\begin{equation}
  u_i = P_i(<\theta_i),
\end{equation}
so that every training point lies in the unit hypercube defined by $0<u_i<1$.
This is the space in which the Sobol design was constructed, and is therefore the space in which the training data are most nearly space filling.
It also avoids asking the GP to learn across raw parameters with very different units, ranges, and prior shapes; a fixed distance in $u_i$ corresponds to the same amount of prior probability for each parameter.

Before fitting each GP, the target coefficient is standardised by subtracting its training-set mean and dividing by its training-set standard deviation.
The kernel specifies the covariance between the values of the function at two points in parameter space.
We use a Mat\'ern kernel with $\nu=2.5$, multiplied by a constant amplitude, and allow a separate length scale for each input parameter.
For two points in prior-quantile space, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{u}'$, the kernel is
\begin{equation}
  k(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}')
  =
  A
  \left(1+\sqrt{5}r+\frac{5r^2}{3}\right)
  \exp\left(-\sqrt{5}r\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  r^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{u_i-u_i'}{\ell_i}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
Here $A$ is the covariance amplitude and $\ell_i$ is the length scale associated with the $i$th \galacticus{} parameter.
Small values of $\ell_i$ allow the emulator prediction to vary rapidly as that parameter is changed, while large values correspond to a smoother, less sensitive dependence.
For each PCA coefficient, the kernel hyperparameters are therefore the overall covariance amplitude and the 20 length scales describing how rapidly that coefficient changes as each \galacticus{} parameter is varied in prior-quantile space.
We choose these hyperparameters by maximising the GP log marginal likelihood \citep{2006gpml.book.....R}.
In practice this maximisation is carried out with the L-BFGS-B algorithm, subject to the bounds on the kernel amplitude and length scales.
Because the log marginal likelihood can have multiple local optima, we repeat the optimisation from four additional starting points, randomly drawn within the allowed hyperparameter bounds, and keep the solution with the largest log marginal likelihood.

\subsubsection{Emulator Training Uncertainties}

Where available, we include the estimated uncertainty in the training outputs by adding a diagonal noise term to the GP covariance matrix \citep[e.g.][]{2006gpml.book.....R}.
In the notation used by \texttt{scikit-learn}, this diagonal variance contribution is the \texttt{alpha} parameter; here we refer to its square root as the training-output uncertainty.
These uncertainties are provided by the \galacticus{} output analyses for each binned observable, using the covariance model associated with that analysis.
For example, the stellar mass function analysis uses a binomial covariance model rather than a simple Poisson error estimate.
For a bin-by-bin emulator, if the transformed observable value in bin $b$ has training-output uncertainty $\sigma_b$, the corresponding variance $\sigma_b^2$ is passed directly to the GP for that bin.
For the PCA emulator, we instead need an approximate variance for each PCA coefficient.

Let $y_b$ be the transformed observable in bin $b$, and let the standardised observable be
\begin{equation}
  z_b = \frac{y_b-\mu_b}{s_b},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_b$ and $s_b$ are the mean and standard deviation used in the pre-processing step.
If the variance of $y_b$ is $\sigma_b^2$, then the variance of $z_b$ is
\begin{equation}
  {\rm Var}(z_b) = \frac{\sigma_b^2}{s_b^2}.
\end{equation}
The PCA coefficient for component $k$ is
\begin{equation}
  a_k = \sum_b e_{kb}\,(z_b-\bar{z}_b),
\end{equation}
where $e_{kb}$ is the $b$th element of the $k$th PCA component.
Neglecting covariance between bins in the training uncertainty, we assign this coefficient the variance
\begin{equation}
  {\rm Var}(a_k) \simeq \sum_b e_{kb}^2\,\frac{\sigma_b^2}{s_b^2}.
\end{equation}
This is the variance used as the diagonal noise term when fitting the GP for PCA coefficient $a_k$.

The distinction between emulator uncertainty and model uncertainty is important for the calibration.
A GP prediction has both a mean and a predictive variance.
The predictive variance is small near well-measured, densely sampled regions of the training set, and grows where the training data are sparse, noisy, or require extrapolation.
When this uncertainty is included in the likelihood, an imprecise emulator should therefore weaken the constraints on the \galacticus{} parameters rather than force the MCMC towards a spuriously precise answer.
This is only true if the emulator uncertainty is reasonably calibrated, which is why the held-out validation tests in Section~\ref{sec:emulator-validation} are a necessary part of the inference rather than only a technical check.

\subsubsection{Held-Out Emulator Validation}
\label{sec:emulator-validation}

We validate the emulator using \emph{five-fold cross-validation}.
The \galacticus{} training runs are divided into five equally sized subsets.
For each fold, the emulator is trained on four subsets and evaluated on the remaining subset, so that every \galacticus{} evaluation is predicted once by an emulator that was not trained using that model.
Unless otherwise stated, all validation results in this section are based on the combined predictions from these five folds.

This has two purposes.
First, it checks that the emulator can reproduce direct \galacticus{} calculations at new parameter values.
Second, it lets us assess how choices made in constructing the emulator affect its predictive accuracy, including both the size of the training set and the representation of the observable vectors.
Section~\ref{sec:smf-example} illustrates this validation procedure for the low-redshift stellar mass function.

To quantify this comparison, we consider individual bins of the observable vector.
For a given bin, we compare the emulator prediction, $m_i$, with the direct \galacticus{} value, $g_i$, for each validation model $i$.
The root-mean-square error,
\begin{equation}
  {\rm RMSE} = \left[\frac{1}{N_{\rm val}}\sum_i (m_i-g_i)^2\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
measures the typical vertical scatter about the one-to-one relation between emulator and direct \galacticus{} predictions.
We also use the coefficient of determination,
\begin{equation}
  R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (m_i-g_i)^2}{\sum_i (g_i-\bar{g})^2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{g}$ is the mean direct \galacticus{} value over all validation models.
This compares the emulator error with the intrinsic variation of the \galacticus{} predictions in that bin: $R^2=1$ corresponds to perfect predictions, while $R^2=0$ corresponds to doing no better than predicting the mean \galacticus{} value for all parameter choices.
For the stellar mass functions shown below, both $m_i$ and $g_i$ are values of $\log_{10}\Phi$ in a fixed stellar-mass bin, so the RMSE is measured in dex.

\subsection{MCMC Fits Used In This Work}
\label{sec:mcmc-fits}

We use two kinds of MCMC fit.
First, we run a small set of diagnostic fits to subsets of the calibration data to illustrate how different observables constrain different combinations of \galacticus{} parameters.
The example shown in Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy-breaking} uses stellar mass functions, size--mass relations, and the star-formation-rate function, both separately and jointly.
Second, we run the final joint calibration using the full set of calibration observables summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:observable-summary}, including the Sobral \ha{} luminosity functions.
The final emulators are trained on the 1024-run Sobol campaign described in Section~\ref{sec:emulator-training-set}; no posterior-focused \galacticus{} evaluations are added to the emulator training set.
The final joint fit samples the 20 \galacticus{} parameters in Table~\ref{tab:parameters} together with the five dust parameters described in Appendix~\ref{app:dust-model-details}.

\subsection{The Likelihood}
\label{sec:likelihood}

The MCMC likelihood is evaluated in the same transformed observable space in
which the emulators are trained.  For stellar mass functions, the star
formation rate function, and the \ha{} luminosity functions, this is
$\log_{10}$ number density.  For the size--mass relations and black-hole
relation, the fitted quantities are already logarithmic quantities,
$\log_{10} R_{\rm eff}$ and $\log_{10} M_{\rm BH}$, respectively.  For the
mass--metallicity relation, the fitted quantity is $12+\log_{10}({\rm O/H})$.

For each observable bin $i$, we assume a Gaussian likelihood in this
transformed space,
\begin{equation}
  s_i^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})
  =
  \sigma_{{\rm data},i}^2
  +
  \sigma_{{\rm emu},i}^2(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \ln \mathcal{L}
  =
  -\frac{1}{2}
  \sum_i
  \left[
    \frac{\left(d_i - m_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^2}
         {s_i^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
    +
    \ln\left(2\pi s_i^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)
  \right].
\end{equation}
Here $d_i$ is the target value, $m_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the emulator
mean prediction, $\sigma_{{\rm data},i}$ is the observational uncertainty,
and $\sigma_{{\rm emu},i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the GP predictive
uncertainty.  The data and emulator uncertainties are therefore added in
quadrature in the transformed observable space.

Some of the \galacticus{} analysis outputs store full covariance matrices for the finite-volume uncertainty in the training predictions, and some also store full target covariance matrices.
The final MCMC likelihood uses only the diagonal elements of these matrices, and therefore neglects correlations between bins and between different observable blocks.
The observational uncertainties for logarithmic number densities are obtained by propagating the diagonal linear-space covariance into log space.
A minimum uncertainty floor of $10^{-3}$ is imposed in the transformed observable space.
The final calibration combines 113 non-\ha{} observable bins with 55 \ha{} luminosity-function bins.
The \ha{} likelihood is evaluated in $\log_{10}[\Phi/({\rm Mpc}^{-3}\,{\rm dex}^{-1})]$, using the Sobral et al. target uncertainties propagated into this log-space quantity.
Emulator uncertainties are included for all observable blocks in the final calibration.

For number-density observables whose target uncertainties are supplied in
linear space, we convert the diagonal uncertainty to log space using
first-order propagation,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\log_{10}\Phi}
  \simeq
  \frac{\sigma_\Phi}{\Phi\ln 10}.
\end{equation}
For the Sobral et al. \ha{} luminosity functions we instead use the tabulated
uncertainties in $\log_{10}\Phi$ directly.

This gives a simple Gaussian likelihood in the transformed observable space.
The approximation is most appropriate when the fractional uncertainties are
moderate; sparsely populated number-density bins have intrinsically asymmetric
counting uncertainties, which are only approximately represented by a symmetric
Gaussian error bar in $\log_{10}\Phi$.

\subsection{MCMC Sampling And Convergence}
\label{sec:mcmc-sampling}

We sample the posterior distribution using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), implemented with the affine-invariant ensemble sampler \emcee{} \citep{ForemanMackey:2013io}.
Each MCMC uses 128 walkers, initialized in a small region around a point expected to lie close to the maximum of the posterior density.
This point is determined from a preliminary \emcee{} run with 128 walkers and 75,000 steps.
During an initial burn-in phase, the walkers rapidly disperse from this small region and explore the posterior distribution.
Initializing the walkers in this way reduces the likelihood of walkers becoming trapped around low-probability local maxima.

For the production runs, we use 250,000 steps per walker, discarding the initial 25,000 steps as burn-in.
We assess convergence using the integrated autocorrelation time, $\tau$ \citep{Sokal1997}, estimated separately for each sampled parameter.
For the diagnostic fits used to illustrate parameter degeneracies (see Fig.~\ref{fig:combining_constraints}), the post-burn-in chains extend for at least 25 times the largest measured autocorrelation time.
For our final calibration, which jointly fits all calibration observables, the corresponding ratio is 73.
We therefore consider the chains sufficiently long for the posterior inferences presented here.

The emulator predictions are used in place of direct \galacticus{} evaluations.
This dramatically speeds up inference, because calling the emulator takes a few milliseconds, while a complete \galacticus{} evaluation takes hundreds of CPU hours.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What Emulator-Assisted Calibration Enables}

The emulator makes posterior inference cheap enough to repeat for different combinations of calibration data.
This allows us to compare subset fits, identify which observables drive particular parameter constraints, and locate tensions that would be difficult to diagnose with direct \galacticus{} evaluations alone.
In future applications, the same framework can be extended by adding new observable blocks and retraining or augmenting the relevant emulators.

\subsection{Comparison With \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N}-Body Merger Trees}
\label{sec:nbody-comparison}

The calibration runs use extended Press--Schechter trees because they allow controlled, repeatable sampling of the halo mass function.
This choice is not meant to imply that EPS trees capture every aspect of halo assembly in an $N$-body simulation.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be kept explicit.
First, the emission-line luminosity functions used here are part of the calibration data, so Roman number-density predictions are not independent tests of the \ha{} luminosity function.
Second, the emulator is trained over a finite parameter volume, and posterior inferences near prior boundaries or poorly sampled regions should be treated cautiously.
Third, the likelihood assumes a particular treatment of observational covariance, emulator uncertainty, and possible systematics between heterogeneous datasets.
Fourth, the final mock catalogues will depend not only on the calibrated \galacticus{} model but also on lightcone construction, survey selection, spectral contamination, detector effects, and image simulation.

\subsection{Future Work}

The next step is to populate a $4\times4\,{\rm deg}^2$ lightcone with mock galaxies drawn from the calibrated model and pass the resulting catalogue through Roman grism image simulations.
Those image-level mocks will provide a public downstream data product and will support analysis development within the Roman Project Infrastructure Team.

Inference being cheap allows one to try fitting to different subsets of calibration data, and see where tensions arise, as done in \citet{2026MNRAS.546ag198B}.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item We constructed a 20-parameter emulator training campaign of 1024 \galacticus{} evaluations for galaxy-population and emission-line mock calibration, using a scrambled Sobol design and extended Press--Schechter trees with controlled sampling of the $z=0$ halo mass function.
\item We built PCA-GP emulators of binned observable vectors, using prior-quantile inputs, standardised observable outputs, PCA compression, and Gaussian-process regression of the retained coefficients.
\item Held-out \galacticus{} evaluations and training-size tests validate the emulator for posterior inference over the calibration observables.
\item The calibration constrains the model using stellar mass functions, the star-formation-rate function, size--mass relations, the black-hole--halo-mass relation, the mass--metallicity relation, and \ha{} luminosity functions.
\item Adding \ha{} luminosity functions produces a final calibrated model suitable for Roman grism mock-catalogue generation, with direct \galacticus{} MAP runs confirming the emulator-based solution.
\item The calibrated posterior provides the starting point for future lightcone and image simulations of the Roman grism survey.
\end{enumerate}
